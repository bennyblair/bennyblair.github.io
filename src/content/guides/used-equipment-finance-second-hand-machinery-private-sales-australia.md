# Used Equipment Finance in Australia: Funding Second-Hand Machinery and Private Sales

Used equipment finance is commercial funding used by Australian businesses to buy second-hand machinery, vehicles, plant, tools, or specialist assets. It can apply to dealer purchases and some private sales, but lenders usually need clearer evidence of asset identity, value, condition, ownership, and business use than they would for a standard new-equipment purchase.

A second-hand asset can be commercially sensible when it preserves cash, reduces the purchase price, or helps a business expand without waiting for new stock. The finance question is whether the asset is identifiable, saleable, fit for purpose, and supported by documents that make the lender comfortable.

This guide explains how used equipment finance works in Australia, what lenders assess, when private sales are harder, and what business borrowers should prepare before applying. It is general information only and not financial advice.

## Citation-Ready Answer: What Is Used Equipment Finance?

Used equipment finance is commercial asset finance arranged to help a business buy second-hand equipment, machinery, vehicles, or plant. Lenders usually assess the asset's age, condition, resale value, serial numbers, PPSR status, purchase documents, vendor ownership, business use, borrower trading history, and repayment source. Dealer purchases are often simpler than private sales because invoices, warranties, GST treatment, and asset records are easier to verify. Private-sale equipment can still be financeable where ownership, value, and condition are clearly documented.

## When To Use Used Equipment Finance

Used equipment finance may make sense when the asset will generate revenue, reduce operating costs, replace failed equipment, or support a new contract. A second-hand purchase can be practical if the asset is reliable and the price is materially lower than buying new.

It can also help preserve cash. Instead of paying the full purchase price upfront, the business may spread the cost over time and keep cash available for wages, stock, tax, insurance, or project mobilisation.

## When Not To Use Used Equipment Finance

Used equipment finance may be unsuitable where the asset is too old, too specialised, hard to resell, poorly documented, or priced above market value. Lenders need confidence that the equipment exists, has commercial utility, and can be valued.

It may also be unsuitable where the business cannot show how the asset will be used or repaid. Buying equipment will not solve a weak trading position unless the asset has a clear role in revenue, cost reduction, or operational continuity.

Be careful with urgent private sales where the seller cannot prove ownership, refuses proper documentation, or pressures the buyer to skip checks. A low price is not useful if the asset is encumbered, defective, stolen, or unsuitable for the business.

## Dealer Purchases Versus Private Sales

Dealer purchases are usually easier for lenders because the paperwork is cleaner. A dealer can normally provide a formal tax invoice, asset description, ABN details, warranty information where applicable, serial numbers, and payment instructions.

Private sales can be workable, but they need more verification. The lender may ask for vendor identification, proof of ownership, clear photos, serial numbers, registration details where relevant, a signed sale agreement, and evidence that the vendor has authority to sell.

The private-sale challenge is not just credit. It is asset control. A lender needs to know exactly what is being bought, whether anyone else has a registered interest, and whether the price is reasonable for the asset's age and condition.

## What Lenders Assess

| Assessment area | Why it matters | Evidence that helps |
|---|---|---|
| Asset identity | The lender must know exactly what is being funded. | Serial numbers, VIN, make, model, year, photos, registration, inspection report. |
| Asset value | Used assets can be over- or under-priced. | Dealer quote, comparable listings, valuation, auction results, market evidence. |
| Asset condition | Condition affects resale value and business usefulness. | Photos, service records, inspection, hours, kilometres, maintenance history. |
| PPSR position | Existing security interests can block clear ownership. | PPSR search, payout letter, release confirmation if encumbered. |
| Vendor legitimacy | Private-sale fraud and ownership issues create risk. | Vendor ID, ABN if applicable, bank details, sale agreement, ownership evidence. |
| Business use | The asset should support commercial activity. | Contract, job pipeline, business explanation, revenue link, equipment need. |
| Repayment source | The facility must fit the business cash cycle. | Bank statements, financials, BAS, contracts, management accounts. |

## PPSR Checks Matter

A Personal Property Securities Register search helps identify whether a security interest may already exist over the equipment. This is important because a seller may not have clear title to sell the asset free of debt.

For vehicles and registered plant, identifiers such as VIN, chassis number, or serial number matter. If the asset is encumbered, the lender may need a payout process so the existing secured party is paid and the interest is released.

Borrowers should not treat PPSR as a box-tick. It is part of proving that the asset can be safely bought and financed. Where the position is unclear, obtain professional advice before committing funds.

## Asset Age, Condition and Resale Value

Used equipment is not all assessed the same way. A late-model excavator with service records and an active resale market is different from highly specialised machinery with limited buyers.

Lenders may be more cautious where equipment is old, imported without clear compliance history, missing serial numbers, heavily modified, or hard to inspect. They may also reduce advance rates or ask for a deposit where resale value is uncertain.

If the business is buying equipment because it is cheap, prepare evidence that the asset is still fit for use. Photos, service records, maintenance history, machine hours, independent inspections, or comparable listings can improve the file.

## Deposit, Equity and Loan Structure

Some used equipment purchases require a borrower contribution. The deposit can reduce lender risk and help cover uncertainty around condition, value, GST treatment, or private-sale documentation.

The finance structure may vary by asset type, business profile, security, and lender appetite. Some transactions are straightforward asset finance. Others may need a broader business finance structure if the asset is specialised, the vendor is private, or timing is compressed.

## Documents To Prepare

A lender-ready used equipment file usually includes:

- buyer entity details, ABN, and business purpose;
- invoice, quote, or signed sale agreement;
- seller details and payment instructions;
- make, model, year, serial number, VIN, registration, hours, or kilometres;
- photos of the asset and compliance plates where relevant;
- PPSR search and payout details if the asset is encumbered;
- valuation evidence or comparable listings;
- service history, inspection report, or maintenance records if available;
- recent business bank statements, BAS, or financials;
- explanation of how the asset supports revenue or operations.

The file should tell a simple story: what is being bought, who owns it, what it is worth, why the business needs it, and how the finance is repaid.

## Example Scenario

A civil contractor wins a new project but needs an additional excavator. A new machine has a long delivery window, while a second-hand unit is available from another contractor.

The lender asks for the sale agreement, vendor ID, serial number, photos, service records, PPSR search, comparable listings, and evidence of the new project. If the machine is identifiable, reasonably valued, and useful for the contract, the file is easier to assess.

If the vendor cannot prove ownership or the PPSR search shows unresolved debt, the transaction may need to pause until the title position is fixed.

## FAQ

### Can used equipment be financed in Australia?

Used equipment can be financed in Australia where the asset is identifiable, valuable, suitable for business use, and supported by proper documents. Lender appetite depends on asset type, age, condition, vendor, borrower profile, and repayment source.

### Is private-sale equipment finance possible?

Private-sale equipment finance may be possible, but it usually needs stronger verification than a dealer purchase. Lenders may ask for vendor ID, proof of ownership, serial numbers, PPSR checks, photos, a sale agreement, and valuation evidence.

### Why do lenders care about PPSR searches?

Lenders care about PPSR searches because another lender or secured party may already have an interest in the asset. A clear or properly discharged PPSR position helps show the buyer can obtain the asset without unresolved security interests.

### Does asset age affect used equipment finance?

Asset age can affect used equipment finance because older assets may have lower resale value, higher maintenance risk, and fewer interested lenders. Service records, inspections, and comparable sales evidence can help support the file.

### Do I need a deposit for used equipment finance?

A deposit may be required where the asset is older, specialised, privately sold, hard to value, or outside a lender's standard appetite. The required contribution depends on the transaction and lender assessment.

### What documents are needed for second-hand machinery finance?

Common documents include a quote or sale agreement, asset details, serial numbers, photos, PPSR search, valuation evidence, vendor details, business bank statements, BAS, and an explanation of how the machinery will be used.

No. Used equipment finance information is general commercial lending information only. Business owners should obtain appropriate accounting, tax, legal, and financial advice before buying or financing business assets.
