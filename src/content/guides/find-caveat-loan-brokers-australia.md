## What a caveat loan broker actually does

A broker gathers the transaction facts, identifies potential lender fit, prepares a submission, coordinates information and helps compare terms. The lender sets conditions, makes the credit decision and, if it proceeds, provides funds.

business.gov.au notes that you can use a business loan broker to help you find the right loan. A good broker will understand your business needs, recommend some loan options and handle most of the paperwork for you. Some brokers charge for their services, while others are free and get a commission from the lender.

For a caveat or other short-term property-backed commercial file, broking also means identifying title issues, existing debts, the use of funds, legal dependencies and the exit before approaching lenders. A broker who only forwards an address and a requested amount is not working the file.

## How a broker works a caveat file

A useful broker process is the same whether the firm is Emet Capital or another broker. The order below is a working sequence, not a promise of speed or approval.

1. **Facts.** Confirm the legal borrowers, directors, trustees and guarantors; the exact amount needed; the required date; and who will sign.
2. **Title and security.** Establish ownership, existing mortgages and caveats, ranking, consent issues and whether a caveat structure is even a lawful and practical security method for this property.
3. **Purpose.** Document a genuine business purpose. Consumer or household use is outside this market.
4. **Exit.** Identify the primary repayment event, the evidence already in hand, remaining steps and a fallback if that event is late.
5. **Lender fit.** Explain which lender types may be able to assess this combination of property, title, purpose and exit—and which facts would exclude others.
6. **Submission.** Send one consistent pack. Incomplete or contradictory versions sent to several lenders make the file harder to assess.
7. **Terms comparison.** Compare written terms on the same assumptions: net proceeds, total cost, conditions, dependencies and what happens if the exit slips. An informal indication is not a credit decision.

The broker’s job is to prepare that comparison. The lender’s job is to decide whether to offer, on what conditions, and whether to fund.

## Check identity, ABN and authorisation

business.gov.au tells borrowers to check who they’re dealing with. To make sure it’s a real company, search for the name or ABN on the Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC) register.

ASIC’s [professional registers search](https://www.asic.gov.au/online-services/search-asic-registers/professional-registers-search/) lets users search several registers at once to find out about a person or an organisation registered or licensed with ASIC to provide a service. Those registers include credit licensees and credit representatives. You can search by individual or company name, registration or licence number, ACN or ABN. Before you choose a professional, ASIC says you may want to check the professional is registered or licensed and the services they are licensed to provide.

ASIC also explains [credit-representative arrangements](https://www.asic.gov.au/for-finance-professionals/credit-licensees/credit-representatives/), including that an authorisation is of no effect if the credit activity is not covered by the licensee’s licence.

Commercial and business-purpose lending can involve different legal requirements from consumer credit. Do not infer from a register search alone that every activity is authorised—or that an absent result proves wrongdoing. Ask the broker to state the legal entity, role, and the credit licence, credit representative status or exemption relied on for this file, and obtain independent legal advice where the position is unclear.

## Questions to ask before appointing a broker

1. Who is the legal broking entity and who will handle the file?
2. What experience do you have with this security, purpose and deadline?
3. Which lender categories may fit, and why?
4. What information is needed before you discuss timing?
5. What broker, lender, valuation, legal and third-party costs may apply?
6. How are you paid and what conflicts should I understand?
7. What conditions commonly stop or delay a caveat loan?
8. What is the proposed primary exit and fallback?
9. Will I receive lender terms and documents in writing?
10. What happens if the valuation or consent position changes?

## What the broker should request from you

A credible initial pack usually includes:

- borrower, director, trust and guarantor details;
- exact use of funds and required date;
- property address, ownership and current title information;
- current mortgage, caveat and other debt balances;
- recent valuation or property evidence, if available;
- business financials, bank statements and tax position;
- creditor, contract or settlement documents supporting urgency; and
- an evidenced repayment, refinance or sale exit.

A broker who submits incomplete or contradictory versions to multiple lenders can make the file harder to assess.

## Compare the broker process, not just one term

business.gov.au says it’s a good idea to compare loan products from multiple lenders before applying, and to read each loan’s terms and conditions carefully.

| Comparison point | What to record |
|---|---|
| Proposed lender | Legal lender, funding source and decision-maker |
| Facility | Amount, term, security, repayment and drawdown |
| Total cost | Interest, lender fees, broker fees, valuation, legal and exit costs |
| Conditions | Valuation, consent, identity, legal and settlement requirements |
| Timing | Dependencies still outstanding, not a marketing promise |
| Exit | Primary path, fallback and cost if delayed |

Ask whether every quote uses the same facility amount, security and exit date. A cheaper-looking indication is not comparable if it assumes a different property, a cleaner title or a faster exit than the file actually supports.

## Warning signs

- promises of approval, a named rate or a settlement date before the file has been assessed;
- pressure to sign before receiving full costs;
- instructions to misstate the loan purpose;
- reluctance to identify the lender or broking entity;
- unexplained upfront payments to personal accounts;
- no written privacy, engagement or fee documents;
- no questions about repayment or exit; and
- advice to ignore existing mortgage or legal obligations.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does a caveat loan broker do that a lender does not?

A broker prepares the file, identifies possible lender fit and helps compare written terms. The lender makes the credit decision, sets conditions and, if it proceeds, provides the funds. A broker who speaks as if they are the funder is blurring that line.

### Does a specialist broker produce a better rate?

No. Experience may improve lender matching and file preparation, but final terms depend on lender assessment and the transaction. A specialist label is not a pricing promise.

### Should I use several brokers at once?

Multiple uncoordinated submissions can create duplication and conflicting information. Ask how the broker will approach the market and require approval before distributing sensitive documents.

### Can a broker confirm timing before valuation?

They can identify a possible process and the remaining dependencies. Valuation, title, consent, legal documents and lender credit may still change timing. A date given before those items are reviewed is not a decision-useful timetable.
